### Grain proof

A table is only safe to join once you know whether its key identifies a row. Keys
marked **unique** must not repeat, and the gate fails if they do. The rest are *join*
keys: they repeat by design, and what matters is the fan-out — rows per distinct key,
and the worst single key. That multiplier is how far wrong a `count(*)` after a join
would be.

Where the key is not an identity, and why:

- `faers_drug` — one row per reported lot/dose of a drug — join key, not identity.
- `faers_reac` — the same reaction term can be reported more than once per case.
- `faers_ther` — one row per therapy interval; a drug can have several.
- `faers_indi` — duplicate indication rows occur upstream.

The sharpest case is `faers_drug`: one key accounts for 103 rows. Joining on it and counting rows would report that many of what is really one thing. Counting distinct keys, or aggregating before the join, is the fix.

## FAERS case-version dedup

A FAERS case is reported once and amended afterwards, and each amendment ships as a
new row in a later quarter. Counting rows therefore counts amendments, not events.
The resolution rule is the FDA's: latest `fda_dt`, ties broken on the highest
`primaryid`, then on the quarter — a total order, so the result is reproducible.

| | |
|---|---:|
| Demo rows loaded | 1,643,483 |
| …carrying a case id | 1,643,483 |
| Distinct cases kept | 1,533,688 |
| Versions superseded | 109,795 |
| Cases the FDA later deleted | 4,152 |

Kept + superseded = 1,643,483, against 1,643,483 demo rows
with a case id — they balance.
Every row is either a surviving version or one it superseded; none is lost to the
dedup and none is counted twice.

In practice the raw extract over-counts distinct cases by 1.07×. An analysis that
skips this step reports that much more adverse-event activity than was reported.

## Partial dates kept, not guessed

FAERS date columns are variable-width: `20250326` is a day, `200906` a month with no
day, `2009` a year. Padding the missing parts invents values that then cannot be
told from observed ones. Each date lands as three columns instead — `*_raw`,
`*_prec` and a real `DATE` populated only at day precision.

## Cross-table integrity

Child rows whose parent is missing, scoped to the child's own partition: a FAERS
drug row belongs to the demo row in its own quarter, not to any quarter. Written as
`NOT EXISTS` — a `LEFT JOIN` filtered in `WHERE` silently becomes an inner join and
reports zero orphans whether or not there are any.

An orphan is **explained** when the parent row was rejected at load time: that child
was always going to point at nothing, and the reject lane says so by name. It is
**unexplained** when the parent simply is not in the feed, which is an upstream
inconsistency. Only unexplained orphans fail the gate.

## What this does not show

- **Not a pharmacovigilance signal.** FAERS reports are voluntary, unverified and
  duplicated across reporters; a case being present says someone reported it, not that a
  drug caused it. Nothing here computes a disproportionality statistic, and the counts
  cannot be used as incidence rates.
- **Point-in-time.** Both sources are pinned archives. The numbers above describe the
  loaded partitions, not the current state of either registry.
- **A documented column subset.** `aact_studies` keeps 31 of `studies.txt`'s 71 columns;
  the rest are result-reporting and IPD-sharing metadata nothing here joins on.
- **Gates, not profiling.** They prove keys, conservation and referential integrity.
  They say nothing about whether a value is clinically plausible.

## What the first full load actually taught me

Three things that are only visible once the gates run over real volume.

**The grain I declared for FAERS was wrong, and the gate is how I found out.** The documentation
implies `(primaryid, drug_seq)` identifies a drug row. It does not: 7.3M drug rows carry only 6.6M
distinct keys, and the worst key accounts for 103 rows that differ in nothing but the reported
manufacturing lot. That is a join key, not an identity, and the difference is a silent 1.11×
multiplier on any `count(*)` taken after joining drugs to therapy dates or indications. The fix was
to correct the declaration and publish the fan-out, not to widen the key until the gate went quiet.

**Partial dates are not a rounding error.** 26.8% of therapy start dates and 18.5% of end dates are
month- or year-precision. Padding them to a `DATE` — the usual shortcut — would have fabricated a
day for roughly 412,000 therapy start dates and then made them indistinguishable from the 1.1M that
were actually observed. Any duration or time-to-onset analysis over this corpus has to filter on
`start_dt_prec = 'day'` and accept a smaller denominator, or say out loud that it did not.

**The reject lane and the integrity gate check each other.** 2,046 lines across the AACT load have
more fields than their header declares, because the export is unquoted and free text contains the
delimiter. 62 of those are studies — and the 857 orphaned sponsor, condition, intervention and
facility rows resolve to exactly those 62 nct_ids. Two independent checks arriving at the same 62
records is the strongest evidence here that both are working. An orphan count that did *not*
reconcile would mean either the reject lane is losing rows or the upstream is referentially
inconsistent, and the gate now distinguishes the two instead of reporting one number.

### What I would check next

- Whether a drug row's identity is recoverable at all — `(primaryid, drug_seq, lot_num)` still
  leaves conflicting keys, so the honest answer may be that FAERS DRUG has no key and consumers must
  always aggregate before joining.
- The 5 invalid `event_dt` values and the 9 invalid `start_dt` values: small enough to read by hand,
  and worth knowing whether they are transpositions or a format this parser does not know about.
- Whether `faers_deleted_case` should propagate — 4,152 cases the FDA has withdrawn are still
  present in `faers_demo` and its children, flagged only on `faers_case_current.is_deleted`. That is
  deliberate (nothing is dropped), but any count over the raw tables includes retracted cases unless
  it joins through.
